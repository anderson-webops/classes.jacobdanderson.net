# Elementary Science Rubrics and Answer Key

## Common Two Band Evidence Rubric

Assess each category as starting, growing, secure, or extending:

- **Phenomenon and source:** identifies what is being studied and where evidence comes from.
- **Observation:** points to, draws, selects, dictates, or writes a direct source detail.
- **Representation:** uses a labeled drawing, sort, table, graph, or model that another person can follow.
- **Explanation:** connects evidence to a claim with age-appropriate reasoning.
- **Boundary:** distinguishes observation, inference, model, prediction, and preference.
- **Revision:** changes an idea or design after comparison, feedback, or test evidence.
- **Accessibility:** provides text equivalents and does not rely on color, audio, handwriting, or physical access alone.

For K–2, secure work can be oral, selected, dictated, or drawn and includes a source detail plus one because connection. For grades 3–5, secure work adds organized data or model evidence, a fuller reasoning chain, and one limitation or changed-condition prediction.

## Science Practices Key

An observation reports what the supplied source shows. An inference is a possible explanation. A claim answers the question. Evidence is a relevant source detail. Reasoning explains why that detail supports the claim.

In Mystery Scene A, puddles, droplets, cloud cover, depth, and time are observations. Rain, a sprinkler, blocked drainage, or uneven ground are possible explanations. The image cannot choose among them without more evidence.

In Mystery Scene B, P grows 4 cm and Q grows 1 cm. The simplified comparison changes the light card while holding the supplied water amount equal. It supports “P grew more in this table,” not “more light always makes every plant grow.”

## Living Things Key

Movement, spreading, using fuel, or becoming larger is not enough to classify something as living. Use multiple life-process criteria and preserve once-living and dormant categories.

Plants use light energy, water, carbon dioxide, and materials from their environment to make and build organic matter. Soil supplies water and minerals but is not food in the everyday sense. Structure-function claims are conditional: webbed feet can support swimming, but one trait does not guarantee survival or prove every habitat relationship.

## Ecosystems Key

The course arrow convention is source of food or energy → receiver. Energy enters most modeled food webs through sunlight and producers, moves through consumers, and disperses as heat. Decomposers transform dead material and waste and return matter to environmental reservoirs; they do not recycle energy.

Heron observation rates are 0.5, 0.625, 0.75, and 0.375 observations per hour. Period C has fewer raw observations but fewer observation hours. Population, encounter rate, and cause remain different claims.

## Weather Water and Earth Key

The seven-day table supports exact weather descriptions and a limited short forecast discussion. It cannot establish climate.

Water pools include ocean, atmosphere, soil, groundwater, ice, and organisms. Flows include evaporation, condensation-related state change, precipitation, runoff, infiltration, and transpiration. The Sun drives much evaporation; gravity drives falling and downhill movement.

Weathering breaks material down, erosion moves it, and deposition places it. A fast event can begin longer-lasting changes, so fast and slow are time-scale descriptions rather than mutually exclusive categories for an entire landscape.

## Matter and Materials Key

Observable properties and particle models answer different questions. Gases are matter. Solid particles move in the model even when a solid keeps its shape. Dissolved salt remains in the system even when grains are no longer visible.

A material recommendation is conditional on criteria and constraints. Clear plastic supports transparency and water resistance; cloth supports absorbency and flexibility; other jobs can favor other options. Reversible classifications state what process and recovery boundary are used.

## Forces and Machines Key

Force arrows represent pushes or pulls on the selected object. Balanced forces mean zero net force in the simplified case, not necessarily zero motion. A position-time graph describes position over time; a flat section means unchanged position, while equal position changes over equal time indicate constant speed.

The machine table shows lower supplied force ratings paired with longer effort distances or setup needs. It supports a force-distance tradeoff, not the claim that machines create energy or remove all work.

## Light Sound and Signals Key

Light travels source → interaction → receiver. Objects are seen when light reaches an eye or camera after emission or reflection. A shadow is a lower-light region formed when an object blocks part of a path.

Sound begins with vibration, travels through matter in the course model, and is detected by a receiver. Pitch and loudness are different features. A reliable signal has a clear message, distinct pattern, intended receiver, and accessible alternative when noise, visibility, color, hearing, or ambiguity creates a barrier.

## Sky and Models Key

Repeated sky observations establish patterns. The day-night model uses Earth rotation to explain changing illumination at one location. The Sun's apparent daily motion is not evidence that it travels around Earth each day.

Solar-system models trade accuracy dimensions. A model can preserve planet order, one relative size comparison, one orbit pattern, or selected surface details while omitting or distorting other features. Planet comparisons use the same property and unit rather than judging from drawing size.

## Capstone Defense Rubric

The capstone answers:

1. What phenomenon, question, or design problem is being studied?
2. Which supplied evidence supports the response?
3. What does the representation show?
4. What is directly observed, inferred, modeled, or predicted?
5. How does the explanation differ for K–2 and grades 3–5?
6. If designing, what are the criteria, constraints, and alternatives?
7. What comparison or test was used?
8. What failed, remained uncertain, or lay outside the model?
9. What changed from the first version to the final version?
10. What evidence would change the explanation or design?
11. Can the work be understood through a text route without color, sound, physical materials, or personal disclosure?

A polished drawing or presentation cannot replace evidence and revision. K–2 work can satisfy the rubric through oral, selected, dictated, or drawn responses. Grades 3–5 work adds organized data, fuller CER reasoning, and a limitation or changed-condition prediction.
